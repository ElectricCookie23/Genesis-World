# Decisions

Choices made where the brief was unclear or something had to change. Newest at the bottom.

1. **Commit messages** carry no Co-Authored-By line and no session link (owner's choice, matches the brief).
2. **GitHub Pages:** the workflow builds, tests and deploys on every push to `main`. The repo is currently private, and Pages on a free plan needs a public repo, so the deploy job is marked `continue-on-error` and will start working once the owner makes the repo public (or upgrades) and sets Settings > Pages > Source to "GitHub Actions". Repo visibility is the owner's call and was not changed.
3. **Speed labels are not literal multipliers.** 1x is one sim day per real second as the brief requires, but a literal 1000x would be under 3 years per second, leaving a 100,000-fold gap to Epoch. Speeds are: 1x = 1 day/s, 10x = 1 year/s, 100x = 30,000 years/s, 1000x = 100,000 years/s, Epoch = 300,000 years/s (100x was raised from 5,000 so that the first life appears within about half a minute of the opening). A full history at Epoch is about 500 million sim years in 28 minutes.
4. **Planet grid:** icosphere at frequency 63 gives 39,692 cells (the nearest to 40,000). Cells are the icosphere vertices; the renderer draws the 79,380 triangles between them, flat shaded.
5. **Elevation is stored in km** relative to a fixed datum, and sea level is a separate number, so ice ages can move the coastline without touching terrain.
6. **`src/sim/simulation.ts` and `src/sim/noise.ts`** were added to the brief's file list: the first holds the whole sim so the worker and the Node tests share one code path, the second holds fbm helpers.
7. **Seasons at high speed:** when a tick is longer than about 0.2 years, the sim uses annual-mean temperature (seasons would only alias). The renderer shows a slow cosmetic day/night rotation at those speeds.
8. **Tests use Vitest** (Node environment, no browser) through `npm test`.
9. **Tick rate:** the worker runs 20 fixed ticks per second; the renderer interpolates.
10. **Auto-resume:** a scheduled task fires every 5 hours into the build session so work continues after a usage limit. HANDOFF.md is the note the next session reads first.
11. **Day and night are cosmetic.** At 1x one sim day lasts one real second, which would strobe the sun, so the visible day lasts 80 real seconds at every speed. Seasons (sun declination) follow the sim at 1x and 10x.
12. **Custom lighting instead of scene lights.** Every material computes its own sun, sky and night light in TSL from shared uniforms. It is cheaper than PBR lights and keeps terrain, ocean, clouds and atmosphere consistent. Real shadow maps for close-up creatures and trees come later.
13. **Scale:** planet radius is 1 scene unit and 1 km of elevation is 0.0056 units, so 8 km peaks read from space. Life is drawn far larger than true scale (snow-globe planet).
14. **Stars are drawn in the sky shader** (hashed cells) rather than as point sprites, so they look the same on WebGPU and WebGL2.
15. **Life is simulated as populations, not individuals.** A "cohort" is one species in one region sharing one genome. This is the statistical layer the brief asks for at high speed, and it is always the source of truth. Individual creatures near the camera will be drawn from the cohorts.
16. **Ecology runs on a coarser grid** (icosphere n=21, 4,412 regions) laid over the 39,692-cell planet grid. Terrain and climate stay per cell; vegetation is painted back onto cells.
17. **One ecological step is 15,000 sim years** (one tick at Epoch). Slower speeds take fractional steps, so history unfolds at the same pace in sim years at every speed. At 1x and 10x populations are effectively frozen, which is right for a day or a year.
18. **Determinism** holds for the same seed and the same sequence of time steps. A seed code replays the same world; changing speed mid-run changes how the steps fall and so can change details of the history.
19. **Oxygen gates the land.** Sea plants slowly oxygenate the air; land is sterile until an ozone layer forms. This is what places land plants around 50 My and land animals after them, without scripting either.
20. **Soft cap of 40 living animal species** (hard cap 64), and 150 plant species, so the tree of life stays readable and the sim stays fast. Big changes in way of life can still found species above the soft cap.
21. **Origin of life is seeded** (first microbes, first alga, first animal at seeded times). Everything after that emerges.
22. **Headless life tests use a small planet** (n=27) and 120 My so that five seeds finish in about five minutes.
23. **Gene flow.** Cohorts of one species in one region merge only when their genomes are close; a distinct line is absorbed by head count without changing the bigger line's genes. Blending them averaged away every local adaptation and nothing ever left the sea.
24. **Flight widens feeding reach** rather than moving it to the canopy, and true wings keep most of the walking ability. Without both, gliders never became fliers.
25. **Intelligence** pays most in social species (shared knowledge finds more food) and its upkeep was lowered, so minds can evolve. The threshold for founding settlements is 0.53 with sociality above 0.35, on land, with a population over 2,000.
26. **Civilization is abstracted** as settlements with population, technology and a belief. Technology drives every stage: paintings, farms and taming, villages, cities, industry, the space age, departure. Wars, plagues, faiths and ruins are events tied into the cause graph.
27. **Plate tectonics is a conveyor model.** Plate outlines stay fixed; every 2 My each cell takes the terrain upstream of it along its plate's rotation, and new ocean floor forms where the source lies on another plate. Life and settlements do not ride along.
28. **Many Tier 2 and Tier 3 items are events plus a light visual**, marked "simplified" in PROGRESS.md with what is missing. The graphics overhaul that follows will replace most of the visuals anyway.
29. **Rewind keeps six points** (a save is about 15 MB), taken at milestone-grade events at least 4 My apart, plus manual fork points. The brief asked for fifty.
30. **Day and night behaviour, chases, herding and migration of individuals are drawn, not simulated.** The populations are the truth; individuals near the camera act out what their species does.
31. **Performance could not be measured on a real GPU** in the build environment (software rendering only, where the page runs at about 1 frame per second). Shader cost was cut where it was clearly wasted (cloud shadows, always-on noise), panels lose their blur below the high preset, and adaptive quality steps down bloom, instance counts and resolution.
32. **GitHub Pages** could not be enabled from the build session (the API path is blocked and the repo is private). The owner needs to make the repo public and set Settings, Pages, Source to GitHub Actions.

## Graphics overhaul (owner's direction, logged before work began)

33. **New art direction:** an alien world, not Earth, and high-fidelity cinematic 3D rather than the low-poly diorama. BRIEF.md section 3 is superseded by `docs/GRAPHICS_BRIEF.md`.
34. **Targets are not downgraded for WebGL2.** The FFT ocean, GPU hydraulic erosion and temporal anti-aliasing are built in full on WebGL2: render-to-texture ping-pong for the FFT and the erosion, a history buffer for TAA. They are not replaced by simpler stand-ins.
35. **Tessellation is replaced by quadtree LOD terrain** (chunked cube-sphere that refines toward the camera).
36. **WebGPU is optional.** A WebGPU path is kept behind feature detection only where it adds quality. WebGL2 must look the same on the High preset.
37. **Screenshots from the build machine check correctness, not final look.** It renders in software. The owner judges the visuals on a real GPU from the deployed site, so every step is pushed as soon as it is correct.
38. **After the graphics overhaul,** return to the items marked "simplified" in PROGRESS.md and complete them.
39. **Expansion brief queued.** `docs/EXPANSION_BRIEF.md` (100 systems) follows after the graphics overhaul and the simplified-items pass, in that order, as the owner asked.
40. **Speciation thresholds are measured in average populations,** not shares of the whole species. As fixed shares they made speciation nearly impossible on the full-size planet (one species after 280 My) while small test planets diversified.
41. **Minds need time.** A lineage must stay above the intelligence threshold (0.5) for 60 My before it founds settlements, and technology grows about seven times slower than first tuned, so civilization starts later and its ages last long enough to watch. At most four population groups share a region (was six) to keep the full-size step fast.
42. **Rivers are part of the ground, not ribbons.** The drainage network is drawn into a cube map as a vector distance field (offset to the nearest river centre line, plus that river's width), so the terrain shader draws smooth, meandering rivers of any width, with wet banks and plants along them, at every zoom.
43. **Terrain shadows are marched through the height map** toward the star in the terrain shader instead of using cascaded shadow maps. On a planet seen from orbit to ground level this gives long mountain shadows at any scale with no shadow-map resolution problems. Ambient occlusion on the ground comes from the height field (hollows and eroded valleys); screen-space GTAO for plants and animals is added with post-processing.
44. **One height function, two places.** The CPU ground sampler (`gfx/ground.ts`) repeats the terrain shader's height (cell elevation interpolated across the triangle, plus the eroded detail read back once from the GPU), so everything placed on the ground sits exactly on what is drawn.
45. **Volumetric clouds are marched in the scene, not as a post pass,** on an inside-out sphere with depth testing off and the scene's depth texture limiting the march. This keeps the atmosphere and aurora drawing over them in the right order. The noise is one 64-cubed tileable 3D texture built on the CPU at load. The simulation now reports cyclone centres so the renderer can draw spiral bands and an eye.
46. **The FFT ocean is verified, not assumed.** The butterfly inverse FFT running in ping-pong targets was compared in the browser against a direct CPU sum of the same spectrum: the height and displacement fields agree to about 0.1%. Three cascades each own a band of wavelengths. Waves are mapped onto the sphere on three planes and blended, so there are no seams or stretching anywhere on the globe.
47. **Wave scale is a visual choice.** One metre of wave tile is 2.2e-5 planet radii (the world is a miniature with exaggerated relief), heights are stretched 1.7 times, and the spectrum is built for an 11 m/s wind and scaled per place by the sim's wind and storm cover.
48. **Water is see-through, with real depth.** The sea surface is drawn after the solid world and reads the scene's colour and depth: it bends the image of the sea bed by the wave normals and absorbs light by the true distance travelled through the water. Shore foam fades in from the same distance, so there is no hard line where water meets land.
49. **Nations are derived by the renderer, not simulated.** The sim keeps settlements; the oldest settlement in a neighbourhood becomes a capital and later towns of the same people within reach belong to it (a shared faith widens the reach, a rival faith narrows it). Names, colours, emblems, territory, borders, roads and sea lanes are all computed from the settlement list on the main thread, so the graphics brief's rule of not changing simulation logic holds. Wars and plagues reach the lights through the ordinary event stream.
50. **Light colour follows biology, then technology.** A nation's signature hue starts from its species' glow or skin hue gene and is spread apart for nations of the same species. Until technology passes about 0.45 every people burns fire (amber); after that its lights shift to the signature colour.
51. **Roads and sea lanes reuse the river technique** (a vector distance field in a cube map), so they are thin, smooth threads at any zoom. Roads are routed with a shortest-path search that prefers gentle slopes, river valleys and coasts.
52. **Plants and grass find the ground on the GPU.** Each instance stores only its direction on the globe; the vertex shader evaluates the same height function the terrain uses. Thousands of plants cost no CPU height lookups and can never float or sink.
53. **Imposters are baked from the real models at load,** from the side, from 45 degrees and from above, read back and assembled into one atlas on the CPU so both backends give the same result. Far plants are one instanced draw call of camera-facing cards that pick the view by the angle they are seen from; near and far versions cross-fade with a screen dither.
54. **Plant form follows genes:** height class, leaf type, water need, glow, fruit and fire resistance choose among twelve growth forms; colour comes from the world's two pigments and the leaf-hue gene, flowers and glow bulbs from the flower-hue gene.
55. **Creatures are skinned in the vertex shader, not with bone matrices.** Each vertex knows its part and how strongly it follows that part; legs are solved with closed-form two-bone IK per vertex, with the foot target placed on the terrain height sampled in the shader. One material serves every species: a species' skin settings (second colour, pattern, cover, wetness, iridescence, glow) ride along as instance attributes, so new species never compile new shaders.
56. **Fur is drawn as shells:** insulated species carry four extra copies of the body skin pushed outward along the normal, cut into strands by an alpha test that thins toward the tips. Only the body skin is shelled, to keep the triangle count down.
57. **Ash and smoke are part of the cloud volume,** not sprites: up to four columns (the hottest eruptions and fires) are added to the density the cloud march already integrates, with their own dark colour and a glow from the fire below. Low smoke near the ground is still particles.
58. **Rain and snow never touch the CPU.** Each streak's position is a function of a per-instance seed, time, fall speed and wind inside a box that travels with the camera, so six thousand drops cost one draw call.
59. **TAA velocity comes from depth, not from a velocity buffer.** Most of the picture passes through large transparent layers (air, cloud, aurora) that would write wrong velocities with additive blending. Instead the previous camera matrix reprojects every pixel from the depth buffer. Moving animals and waves are handled by the history clamp.
60. **Auto exposure meters like a camera, not like a histogram.** A 16x16 grid of the HDR frame is read back every few frames; the target follows mostly the plain average of lit pixels (so cloud and snow are not blown out) tempered by the log average (so dark scenes lift), limited to 0.6x to 3.6x. Pixels of empty space do not count, and the starfield, nebula and star glare are divided by the exposure so space stays dark when the night side is brightened.
61. **AgX replaces ACES** for its gentle highlight roll-off (city lights, lava, the star), with saturation raised by about a fifth afterwards because AgX alone looks flat on this palette.
62. **Plates drift for real now** (replaces decision 27's conveyor). Each plate saves up its rotation until it has moved one cell, then all its crust steps forward together; a cell reached by two plates is a collision (higher crust stays on top, the range grows toward a cap of about 7.5 km), a cell nobody reaches becomes ridge floor and stays with the plate that left. Crust volume lost under collisions is returned as a slow rise of continents and shelves so land does not drain away. Plate speed is applied at 0.6 of the stored value. Life and settlements still do not ride along; a settlement whose ground goes under the sea is abandoned.
63. **GIF and PDF are written by small encoders in the repo** rather than libraries: a fixed 6x7x6 palette with ordered dither and LZW for the GIF; standard fonts and JPEG pictures for the PDF. Both were checked against independent readers.
64. **Small world-shaping effects were kept gentle on purpose.** Flowers get a bonus from pollinators but no penalty without them, and only small ocean islands sink: an earlier, harsher version pushed the coldest test seed under its species floor. Hibernation, symbiosis and nectar are modest multipliers (cold stress x0.55, foraging x1.07, up to +30% intake) so they shape who thrives without rewriting the food web.
65. **Triggers are set from what species really evolve, not from what sounds right.** The first conditions for trails, hibernation, pollination and symbiosis never fired in a 220 My history: migration genes stay below about 0.4, burrowing below 0.3, and powered flight is rare. The gene ranges of living species were printed from real histories and the conditions reset to them. Trails no longer depend on colonising moves (too rare to outpace fading): every few steps each migratory land herd walks between the neighbouring regions it already lives in.
66. **Ocean currents remember.** Sea surface temperature is kept from step to step and pulled toward the water upstream along the wind-driven flow, instead of being recomputed from latitude every step, so heat is carried a long way and coasts get warm or cold currents.
67. **Two futures run as two workers.** "What if" copies the saved world into a second simulation worker with a different random stream; the page shows one and summarises the other, and can cross over. Nothing in the simulation itself knows about it, so determinism per world is unchanged.
68. **Rewind points thin out with age:** recent ones are kept close together and old ones far apart (twelve in all), shown as marks on the timeline strip.
69. **Models share vertex buffers, and self-placed copies carry no matrix.** WebGPU draws with at most 8 vertex buffers and both backends with at most 16 attributes, and the software renderer in the build container enforces neither the way a real GPU does. Attributes are packed into shared buffers (`packAttributes`) and copies placed by the shader are plain counted meshes (`Copies`) rather than `InstancedMesh`. A test holds the limits.
70. **Shader maths is written to be safe for every input, not only the ones that are used.** A masked-out branch still runs on a GPU: the root of a negative strike age made the whole surface not-a-number, and so black, on real hardware while the software renderer drew it correctly.
71. **Preset costs come from a measured machine, and the step-downs protect the picture.** On an M3 Pro only Low held its settings, so High, Medium and Ultra ask for less resolution and fewer cloud steps than first written. When the frame rate still sags, the cloud march thins and resolution eases before bloom, ambient occlusion and (last) anti-aliasing go; the step a machine settles on is remembered per preset, with one cautious try back up when there is room.
72. **Living things keep one scale among themselves.** They are drawn dozens of times larger than life against the terrain so they can be seen, but a big animal's body is about a third of a tall tree, not equal to it.
73. **A real-GPU review now exists** (`docs/GPU_REVIEW.md`, Apple M3 Pro, both backends) and its findings come before new features. The biggest lesson: checks here ran only on the WebGL2 fallback, so WebGPU-only faults went unseen. WebGPU is now checked here too, on Chromium's software adapter (how is in HANDOFF.md). A second session with the owner's GPU fixed and re-tested the undrawn models and the black surface in parallel; where the two overlapped, its verified versions were kept.
74. **Clouds are marched at half resolution** (supersedes the "in the scene" half of decision 45). The march runs as a full-screen pass into a small target before the scene is drawn; the scene then draws that picture on the same shell as before, so the air and aurora still composite over it. Occlusion by ground, trees and animals is tested at composite time, per full-resolution pixel, against the analytic distance to the cloud layer, so silhouettes stay sharp. The march itself no longer reads scene depth; mountains that stand inside the cloud layer are handled by a soft fade. Colour is stored multiplied by cover so enlarging leaves no dark fringes, and a two-ring filter removes the grain.
75. **Non-finite numbers are treated as a class of bug with its own defence** (`render/diagnose.ts`): planet textures are sanitised when baked, shared uniforms are checked every frame, and Shift+D writes out every renderer input. Reason: such a value blacks out land and sea on some graphics cards and is invisible on others, including the software renderer used here.
76. **Deltas need waves.** A rule that only adds mud turned every river into a causeway over millions of years. Mud now settles only in shallow water and is carried off again wherever no river is feeding it.
77. **Animals and their signs are close-up things.** Animals, nest mounds and sound ripples are drawn only from near the ground. From far off they were visual noise, and enlarging them with distance made animals as big as trees.
78. **Minds must be able to evolve** (supersedes the timing in decision 41). In ten reviewed worlds nothing had founded a settlement by 150 My, so nothing built was ever seen. The cause: a brain's benefit and its cost balanced just under the threshold (intelligence 0.49 after 380 My), and then 60 My more were required. Now a social animal shares the cost of a big brain (the quadratic part of its upkeep falls by up to half with sociality), so social lineages keep climbing, and the wait is 15 My. In test histories the first camps appear around 120 to 140 My in most seeds; a cold world of solitary animals may never get there. At most two kinds of mind hold a world at once.
79. **History runs on a human clock** (expansion architecture). The planet steps in units of thousands of years; rulers, wars and inventions need single years. From a camp's first fields onward its nation is advanced by `Civilization.advance` in yearly ticks. Under a fast-forward a call covers many years, so a tick grows (never more than 300 ticks a call) and every rate is scaled to its length: coarse, but bounded, and a whole civilization then passes inside one or two planetary steps, as it would. Farming to orbit takes five to seven thousand years, about four minutes at the new "History" speed of 25 years a second. The worker holds time at that speed while a history is unfolding and says so once; choosing a faster speed lifts the hold.
80. **Prehistory stays on the planet's clock.** Camps outside history still change per ecology step, over thousands of years. Planting fields is the door between the two clocks.
81. **Words are computed, not stored.** A language is a sound inventory shaped by its speakers' bodies, a family seed and a list of sound changes. The word for any meaning is built from the family seed and run through the changes, so daughter languages keep cognates, a name can be re-spoken as it was in any year, and nothing but the change list needs saving. Settlements keep the meanings of their names.
82. **History is plain data.** Nations, cultures, languages, people and houses are plain objects in one `HistoryState`, so saves, rewinds and the two-futures fork carry all of history without special code. Routine successions are recorded on people and houses but are not chronicle events: only the fall of a long house is.
83. **A nation holds what it can reach.** Reach from the capital grows with the era, counted in the spacing of neighbouring towns so it scales with the grid; settlers who go beyond it found a country of their own. This alone gives several nations of different sizes and eras; rise, fall and secession come with system E4.
84. **The place log reserves room for people.** Biome changes may fill 70% of the log; the rest is kept for what was built, owned and abandoned.
85. **Loading refreshes derived data.** Region summaries are not saved; a loaded world ran on year-zero summaries until its next ecology step, which was invisible at planetary speeds and wrong at yearly ones. `loadSimulation` now calls `refreshDerived`.
86. **History that nobody watched is kept as turning points only.** Under a fast-forward a tick spans decades, so rulers are not recorded one by one (a house counts its reigns and the record of "the ruler of the day" is reused), no person or dynasty records are added, and only events of importance 2 or more are kept. Without this a world left running used two gigabytes within 50 My of its first farmers. Watched at the pace of history or slower, everything is recorded in full.
87. **A people that leaves lets its home go quiet** (replaced by decision 97: the home stays lived in). After the departure the towns of the home world empty over a few thousand years, the nations end, and history is over until the species (or another) starts again from camps with its own ages and firsts. This keeps a civilization from idling at the top of the scale for millions of years, and gives each world several histories.
88. **A jump through time stops at the dawn of history** (unless history was already under way), because the next fifteen thousand years are the part worth watching.
89. **The tech web is a web of needs, not of inventions** (E1). Prerequisites and the level of knowledge are counted in needs ("a hard cutting edge"); inventions are ways of meeting a need, and which way a nation takes depends on its land. So a nation without metal is not blocked or behind by definition: it fills the same needs by other, costlier ways, and its level counts only the needs its land can meet at all. Sea knowledge is skipped entirely by a landlocked, riverless people, and whatever depended on it opens without it.
90. **People are made by events, never on a timer** (E6). A general exists because a war began, a scholar because a library burned or a lost craft was worked out again, an explorer because two nations met or settlers set out, a prophet because a plague, war or disaster was fresh in memory. So every person on record has a reason in the cause graph, and the done-when ("every major event names its person") is checked by a test over five whole histories rather than by eye. Rulers are credited where no one else fits (a new age "begins in the reign of", a fallen nation's "last ruler").
91. **Only rulers survive a fast-forward** (E6, follows decision 86). When a tick spans decades no general, prophet or artist is invented, and an event that would have named one says nothing rather than crediting the ruler with a carving or a voyage.
92. **Portraits are drawn, not stored** (E6). A portrait is a pure function of the species' genes, the person's id and traits, the calling, and the age the person rose in (kept on the person as `era`). Five manners by age pair, all SVG, so they cost nothing in the save and look the same on every device.
93. **First meetings and preachers are new kinds of event** (E6): `contact` and `prophet`. Both change the world a little (a meeting adds to what the nation has to study; a preacher moves stability and inequality according to the ruler's nature), which is how people other than rulers bias the simulation.
94. **A young country starts behind, and a small one learns slowly** (E3, revisited while building E6). Measured over five whole histories, neighbours were never more than one age apart until the last few centuries: a new country copied all its parent knew and then researched at nearly the same pace. Now settlers who found a far country leave the newest fifth of the mother country's crafts behind, a breakaway province an eighth (`shed` in `sim/history/tech.ts`, which leaves no memory of a loss, unlike `forget`), and a country with fewer than half the towns of the greatest of its day works at as little as half speed. Gaps of two or more ages now last for centuries in most worlds. `scripts/gaps.ts` measures it.
95. **An age reached twice is not a first twice.** After the last war knocks every nation back, the world's top age is lowered, and climbing back used to announce "The Age of Steam begins" a second time. The history now remembers its peak (`peakEra`); a re-reached age is announced as "The Age of Steam comes again", still a turning point, and marked `again` in the event's data.
96. **A people with boats settles across the water.** Found from the fix session's report that civilization "was gone" on the full-size planet: on the default seed the thinking species lives on an island two regions wide, towns could only be founded on neighbouring land, so the whole history was two towns, never twelve (the space age needs twelve), and its nations churned for ever. Now a town at the water's edge may send settlers across it: the boat holds its heading away from home and lands on the first free shore within the reach of the nation's ships (rafts a strait, sails a narrow sea, keeled ships a wide one, ocean and engine ships anywhere). `voyage` in `sim/civilization.ts`; such towns are marked `overseas`. The default seed now fills its planet: several hundred towns and around a hundred nations by the space age.
97. **History ends in a long peace, not in an empty world** (replaces decision 87). A history cannot be made for ever: the records of nations and people would grow without bound over geological time, and they did (2,390 nations in 30 My under a fast-forward). But emptying the home world after a departure left a planet with no one on it for anyone who fast-forwards, and the cities, roads and Night Map became nearly impossible to see. Now a history settles into the long peace when (a) 1,200 years have passed since a departure (many leave; the towns thin to about half; those who stay keep them lit), or (b) the leading nation has stood at the top of knowledge for 3,000 years without leaving, or (c) 40,000 years have passed since the dawn or since the last time history resumed. In the peace the nations and towns live on as they are, by custom: no rulers are counted, no wars, no research, no new nations; towns still fill free land slowly and can still be lost to the sea, the ice or the death of their species. If a catastrophe takes more than 70 percent of the people the peace is broken: half of what was known is forgotten, leaders are followed again and history resumes. `settle`, `peaceTick`, `unsettle`; `historyLive` is true only while history is being made, and that is what holds time at the History speed and stops a warp.
98. **The last war is rare and remembered.** It was a 4 percent chance in every war once anyone held the sky; with a hundred nations that fired several times per history and ended most of them, and the dieback killed the thinking species itself. Now it needs a nation of the Age of Orbit, happens in about one war in eighty, never after a departure, and never again within 6,000 years of the last one.
99. **The page is told about what is alive.** Every living town and nation is sent; of the dead, only the last 300 ruins and the last 40 nations. A scripted `warp` takes `through: true` to run past the dawn of a history (it otherwise stops there, once per history), and reports the year twice a second while it runs.
100. **Children are counted at the moment of succession, not raised year by year** (E7). A ruler's family exists as numbers until it matters: when the ruler dies the engine decides how many grown children there are (from how long the ruler lived and whether they married), and only the heir, a rival claimant and a consort ever become people on record. This keeps the family tree to the people a chronicle would name.
101. **Marriage is how countries grow without war, and size is how they break** (E7). A marriage gives an alliance for a few generations and a claim that is handed down. A failed house can therefore deliver its country to another family, and close neighbours merge. Without a counterweight this made one country of eighty towns in an age of sail, so a country with more towns than its age can govern (3 in the Age of Camps up to 300 in the Age Beyond) loses stability in proportion, and the existing secessions, civil wars and collapses take it apart.
102. **A branch is a house of its own.** The younger child who wins half a country in a succession war, and the family that inherits a foreign throne, found a new house record that points to the one it came from (`branchOf`), rather than one house ruling two countries. Generations keep counting through a branch.
103. **Preact 11 does not add "px" to numbers in styles.** Found when the family tree collapsed to nothing: `width: 196` is silently dropped. All sizes in styles are written with their unit; older panels that relied on it (margins, the species tree's height) are fixed.
104. **The sky has a human clock too** (E8). The planet's own comet returns every 18 to 34 million years and eclipses were drawn once per few hundred thousand years, so no people would ever have seen either. While history is live, a small comet ("the Visitor", period 46 to 115 years, fixed by the seed) returns on schedule and an eclipse crosses some capital about every 170 years; both are ordinary events in the cause graph, each return of the comet caused by the last. They are what most calendars end up counting from. Nothing is recorded of them under a fast-forward.
105. **A calendar is a small record, and a date is computed** (E8). A count of years is `{ name, own, epoch, eventId, kind }`; a nation points at one. Nothing stores dates: any year is turned into "Year N of X" on demand, in the engine and in the page, so every old event can be shown in the count of the nation it belongs to, including years before Year 1.
106. **Loading rebuilds records a fresh world does not have.** `restore` skipped a nested record when the freshly built simulation had nothing in its place, so anything optional made later in a history (the comet's schedule, the latest sign in the sky) was silently dropped by save, rewind and the two-futures fork. Found by the save-and-load determinism test when E8 added such records; fixed in `sim/snapshot.ts`, with a test.

107. **Goals watch the world; they are not part of it** (owner's direction, 2026-11-20: "a feature where you could play with goals, but for the most part it could be fun and games and chill with no energy or whatever"). A goal is a function of what the page already knows and returns progress, a few words on where things stand, and the event to explain it. There is no mission state in the simulation, no fail state, no unlock order and no cost on any tool, so the sandbox is untouched for anyone who never opens the panel, saves and seed links are unchanged, and a goal reached on one world stays reached on the next. Two goals have two steps (freeze then thaw; fall then recover) and keep one small number per world for that. Targets come from measured runs, not guesses: the first guesses were wrong in both directions (plant cover is counted over the whole globe, so "a third" could never be reached; thirty kinds of animal is reached by 25 My on a full-size planet).
108. **Cold drives people out when it is a change** (E9). The first version moved every nation with towns in a cold country once every 150 years for ever. A town now remembers how warm its land was when its people chose it (`homeTemp`), and only a fall of 4 degrees from that, or ice spreading over land that was founded ice-free, sets them moving; those who stay behind are taken to have chosen the place as it now is.
109. **One culture per species was not enough to mix** (E9). Mixed cultures need different peoples of one species, so peoples now grow apart: a country founded across the sea, or by farmers who began apart from every other nation, is a daughter culture with a language split from its parent and drifted; land colonies and breakaways sometimes are too. A nation keeps the culture of the country it came from otherwise (it used to be looked up by species).
110. **Migrants go where they can walk or sail, to their own kind when that is nearer** (E9). The search is a flood fill over regions that counts water crossings against the reach of the nation's boats. Free land wins unless a nation of the same species is much nearer (or one time in five anyway), which is what produces mixing; a people with nowhere to go dies in place, as before.
111. **A golden age carries the seed of its end** (E10). The conditions that start one (peace, full granaries, partners, a steady country) are the ones its effects erode: wealth gathers in few hands while it lasts, and any war, plague, disaster or shaken succession that reaches the country ends it. So every golden age ends for a reason the chronicle can name and link, and none needs a timer.
112. **Monuments are counted, not yet built** (E10). A great work is an event, an artist and a number on its town. Drawing them on the ground belongs with the town and building work the fix session is doing; the count is in `SettlementInfo.monuments` for whoever draws them.
113. **A town is a plan cut at a size** (E11). The whole plan of a town at its largest (320 plots) is generated once from the place and its history, with a build order on every plot and street. A town of n plots is the first n. This makes growth, the time-lapse and "old centre, new edge" the same thing, keeps every building where it was as the town grows, and costs nothing to store. The brief asks for agent-based growth along desire paths; this first pass plans the roads (toward neighbours, water and temple) instead of treading them, which gives the same look for far less code. Agents can replace the road step later without changing anything downstream.
114. **The plan reads the drawn ground, not the cells** (E11). A cell is as wide as a large town, so shore and slope taken from cells put half a waterfront town in the sea. The plan samples the same ground the terrain shader draws (`GroundSampler`): sixteen bearings outward for the nearest water, and the rise at six plan units for the slope. Buildings, streets and walls are also tested against it, plot by plot.
115. **Streets and walls follow the ground corner by corner** (E11). Buildings stand level on the ground under their middle; a stretch of road or wall doing the same is half buried on any slope and a street reads as a row of dashes. Their vertices each find the ground beneath themselves in the vertex shader.
116. **A replay runs on the wall clock.** The scene's own clock advances by a capped step per frame, so at a few frames a second a sixteen-second time-lapse took minutes. The time-lapse uses `performance.now()`.
117. **Construction is a function of the date** (E12). No town stores what it is building. A crew's job number is the date divided by the crew's span; the plot is a hash of town, crew and job number. This gives every town endless, repeatable building work for the cost of a few hashes, and what you see while watching is what you would see again after a rewind. It is honest about one thing only loosely: the plots being worked on are rebuilt, not strictly the newest ones the town's growth adds.
118. **Job lengths are set for 1x, where a day is a second.** The brief asks that a building can be watched start to finish at 1x. A house takes a season (91 seconds of watching), a hall a year and a half, the largest buildings six years, a great work thirty. A job is only drawn if watching it takes at least three seconds at the present speed, so 10x shows the long jobs and the pace of history shows none.
119. **A rising building is squashed, not cut.** The vertex shader caps each vertex's height at the height the work has reached, so walls rise and the roof closes last, without discarding fragments or a second set of models. Progress travels in the slot a finished building uses for its share of lit windows, as a negative number.
120. **A made-up town on a fresh world is the quick way to look at town drawing.** `scene.setSettlements([...])` with a fabricated settlement draws a full town in ten seconds, where growing a civilization takes two minutes; the simulation does not overwrite it while it has no settlements of its own. Close views in the software renderer run at about one frame a second: keep the window small (720 by 460) or screenshots time out.

121. **Two sessions build the expansion from both ends** (owner, 2026-11-20: "start creating all the features... make sure all have been made and working"). The build session continues in order from Section 2; the fix session builds Section 10 first and works backward. This breaks the brief's "build the sections in order" on purpose: Section 10 leans on the simulation, not on the history systems still to come, and the parts that do (the Last Archive needs the Archive of E52) are built against what exists and marked.
122. **The star system is plain data with its own random stream** (E91 to E97, `sim/cosmos.ts`). It is forked from the seed at the end of world-building and never draws from the other streams, so no existing history changed when it was added (the digest test holds), and the save code carries it without being told. Its rare events have dates fixed by the seed (first flyby after 260 My, first burst after 180 My, the red giant between 650 and 900 My).
123. **"Comet timing ties to the gas giant's orbit" is done at the scale the simulation has.** A giant's year is a dozen home years; a step of the simulation is thousands. So the tie is to the giant's slow cycle (the turning of its orbit), which is given the period the comet already had: the comet returns once per turn, and when a passing planet changes that cycle the comet keeps the new time. The orrery shows the comet's path turned to where the cycle stands.
124. **The neighbour's life is a number, not a second simulation** (E92, simplified). The brief asks for "a simplified evolution sim" on the neighbour; a second world would double the cost for something seen as a tint on a dot. It has an amount, a ceiling set by how kind that world is, and three stages with events. If the Neighbor Link (E98) is built with a real second world, this can use it.
125. **A probe's report is a real world.** `sim.survey(seed)` builds the planet that seed makes on a small grid and reads land, ice, temperature and a 36 by 18 map from it. The seed code is in the report and the link opens that planet, so "a real different seed" can be checked by anyone. Life there is not claimed: nothing has been grown on it.
126. **The red giant ends the world by the rules already there.** It only raises `climate.solar` and takes the sea away; heat tolerance, starvation and the fall of towns do the rest, with one direct dieback as the seas start to boil. The sun's slow brightening after 380 My is unchanged and is now the first part of the same curve.
127. **A message to the watcher is about something the watcher did** (E99). The simulation already keeps the list of the player's interventions (`godEvents`); a message picks the latest one that people was alive for, waits a generation, and says something written for that kind of act. A people cannot speak of what happened before it existed, and each act is answered once, so the messages run out when the meddling does. The signs are a picture of each word (the same word, the same sign), so a player can start to read them.
128. **An archive is made from the cause graph until there is an Archive to make it from** (E100, simplified). Sealing takes the weightiest events on record that involve that people and puts them in order with "years before the end". The finder must be a different people; what is in orbit needs spaceflight, what is in the ground needs a town within about 0.3 of a radius. Peoples are watched town by town each step, so an ending that no single event announces (the last village emptying) still leaves a record.
129. **A people's architecture is a dozen numbers from its genome** (E13). `architectureOf` turns the species' reference genome into proportions (rise, girth), a lift on posts or a sink into the ground, a willingness to wade, roundness, roof pitch, window density, ornament, spacing and scale. The renderer writes them on every building instance (`iForm`, `iStyle`, `iWall`) and the vertex shader reshapes the same seven models; there are no per-species meshes.
130. **Style follows genes that actually vary among thinking species.** Measured over five worlds, the builders all had almost no ornament, armour, fur or extra eyes (costly genes selected away) and were very sociable. Roof pitch is therefore tied mainly to cold and heat tolerance, windows to sight and nocturnality, ornament to curiosity, wall thickness to aggression, and scale to body size, with the showier genes as bonuses.
131. **A sink is a share of height; a lift is a length.** Sunk by a fixed amount, a burrower's hut vanished under the ground. Negative lift means "this share of the building is below ground" (at most half); positive lift is the height of the posts.
132. **A fashion is a function of culture and year; a building remembers only when it was built** (E14). Nothing about style is stored. The page computes a culture's fashion for any year from the culture's number, and a plot's build year from its place in the build order and the town's age. Old centre and new edge then come for free from the plan's build order (E11).
133. **Old towns are capped at sixteen centuries of standing buildings.** In the long peace a town can be millions of years old; mapping that span onto its plots made every building a different period, a speckle instead of quarters. The oldest plot of any town is taken to be 1,600 years old at most, which gives about five fashions across a town.
134. **A link is two towns and a kind; its route is worked out when drawn** (E15). The brief asks for graphs built by cost. The cost decides which towns are joined (distance, climb, water, border), which is what shapes the network; the route between two joined towns is the straight line, drawn from each end for half the way, and lit along the cells it crosses. Storing routes would add thousands of cell lists to every save for little gain at the scale towns are seen from.
135. **Networks change one thing in the simulation so far:** a town on a railway or a wire is not "far" from its capital when a nation's stability is reckoned. That is the piece of "connect to trade and war" that already had a place to go; trade routes and campaigns proper (sections 4 and 5) will use the links when they exist.
136. **Land use is painted from the towns, not stored** (E16). The `cleared` layer is recomputed from the living towns every time the civilization advances, like fields and lights, so it needs no history and vanishes with the town. A second layer remembers what has already been applied to the plant cover, so a change shows at once and is never applied twice whichever of the plants' step and the civilization's runs first.
137. **Deforestation uses the rain model that was there.** Moisture already depended on plant cover (a direct term and recycled humidity downwind). Clearing lowers the cover, and the rain follows, with no new climate code and no new constant to tune. The test fells real forest in a grown world and measures the fall.
138. **A dam's lake is a wide river for now.** The river layer is recomputed by the planet; the civilization raises the flow at a dam and upstream of it each time it paints, to a fixed width, so it never compounds. A drawn lake surface is left for the renderer.
139. **A layer is a building phase or a destruction, kept on the town** (E17). The brief's five stacked cities need towns to be destroyed and refounded five times, which a history rarely does to one spot. Real mounds are mostly one town rebuilt on itself: so each growth into a larger kind of place, each burning, flood, fall of ash and earthquake, each decay and abandonment is a layer of that town, and a new town founded on a ruin adds its own on top. A deep site keeps at most fourteen layers per town (the middle is thinned).
140. **The dig view is its own panel, not the planet's cross-section.** The existing cross-section is a slice through hundreds of kilometres of crust; a town mound is metres. The dig view is opened from a town or a ruin and draws only the site.
141. **A ruin is the same town, less of it** (E18). Decay is a function of the years since a town was left and of what it knew. The renderer draws the ruin from the town's own plan with a falling share of its buildings, each cut off lower by the same height cap that draws a building under construction, in duller colours. No ruin models, nothing stored but the year it was left and the most people it ever held.
142. **The forest returns by the same layer that cleared it.** A ruin keeps a fading share of `cleared` on its own cell and its fields, so regrowth, rain and tree drawing all follow from E16's machinery, and after about 2,600 years the land is exactly wild again.
